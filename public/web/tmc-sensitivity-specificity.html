🕵️ The Medical Test Detective

Solve the Mystery, Not the Sum.

Discover sensitivity, specificity, prevalence and predictive value by investigating imaginary patients—not memorising equations.

Your mission: A test comes back positive. Is the person really diseased? You are the detective. Adjust the clues, inspect 1,000 people, and find out why the same test result can mean different things in different populations.

This is a fictional teaching simulation. The example test and its accuracy figures are illustrative, not clinical guidance. Real decisions depend on validated tests, patient context, and professional guidance.

1. Set the clues

How common is the disease in the population being tested?

Among people who have the disease, how many test positive?

Among people without the disease, how many test negative?

A bigger crowd makes rare events easier to see.

Detective's rule: Change one clue at a time. Keep the others fixed to see what really caused the difference.

4. Four clues that solve the case

Sensitivity · among those who have the disease —

“If disease is present, how often is the test positive?”

Specificity · among those without the disease —

“If disease is absent, how often is the test negative?”

Positive predictive value (PPV) · among positive tests —

“If the test is positive, what is the chance disease is truly present?”

Negative predictive value (NPV) · among negative tests —

“If the test is negative, what is the chance disease is truly absent?”

6. Interview a positive result

Imagine one person has tested positive. The detective asks: what is the chance this person truly has the disease?

Run the population first. Then interview a randomly selected positive or negative test result.

The person is sampled from the relevant group (positive or negative results), not from the whole population. That distinction is the key to predictive value.

8. On-demand revision notes: open a clue

Sensitivity — “catching the disease”

Definition: the proportion of people who have the disease who test positive.

Formula: TP ÷ (TP + FN).

Memory aid: Start with the people who truly have the disease. How many did the test catch?

A highly sensitive test produces fewer false negatives in the tested population. A negative result from a highly sensitive test can help rule out disease in appropriate circumstances, but the clinical context and test limitations still matter.

Specificity — “clearing the healthy”

Definition: the proportion of people without the disease who test negative.

Formula: TN ÷ (TN + FP).

Memory aid: Start with people who do not have the disease. How many did the test correctly clear?

A highly specific test produces fewer false positives among people without disease. A positive result from a highly specific test may help rule in disease in appropriate circumstances, but no test should be interpreted in isolation.

Prevalence — “how common is it before the test?”

Definition: the proportion of a population that has the disease at a specified time or over a specified period.

Formula for this exercise: people with disease ÷ all people in the population.

Prevalence depends on the population being tested. A screening programme for a generally healthy population may have a much lower prevalence than a specialist clinic examining patients with suggestive symptoms.

Positive predictive value — “how much should I believe a positive?”

Definition: the proportion of positive test results that are true positives.

Formula: TP ÷ (TP + FP).

PPV depends on sensitivity, specificity and prevalence. Even a good test can produce more false positives than true positives when the condition is rare enough.

Negative predictive value — “how much should I believe a negative?”

Definition: the proportion of negative test results that are true negatives.

Formula: TN ÷ (TN + FN).

NPV also depends on prevalence. In a low-prevalence population, most people may test negative and most negative results may be true negatives. This does not mean the test is perfect.

False positive and false negative

False positive: test positive despite disease being absent. False negative: test negative despite disease being present.

These errors can cause different harms: unnecessary anxiety, investigations or treatment on one side; delayed diagnosis or missed intervention on the other.

Likelihood ratios — an optional advanced clue

Positive likelihood ratio: sensitivity ÷ (1 − specificity). It describes how much more likely a positive result is in people with disease than in people without disease.

Negative likelihood ratio: (1 − sensitivity) ÷ specificity. It describes the corresponding evidence provided by a negative result.

Likelihood ratios can help update pre-test odds to post-test odds. The game focuses first on natural frequencies because they are easier to visualise.

Screening versus diagnosis

Screening looks for possible disease in people who may not have symptoms. Diagnostic testing evaluates whether disease is present in a particular clinical context.

A screening test's usefulness depends not only on accuracy but also on disease prevalence, follow-up tests, available treatment, harms, costs and whether the screening programme improves outcomes.

Why predictive values change across settings

Sensitivity and specificity are often taught as properties of a test, but their estimates can also vary with disease spectrum, setting, threshold and study design. Predictive values are especially dependent on the prevalence in the population tested.

Do not transfer a PPV from one setting to another without considering whether the underlying prevalence and test performance are comparable.

Bayes' theorem without fear

Bayes' theorem describes how evidence changes our belief about a condition. Natural frequencies provide a friendly route to the same answer:

1. Begin with a known number of people. 2. Estimate how many have disease. 3. Apply sensitivity and specificity. 4. Count true positives among all positive results.

That final fraction is PPV. The game performs the arithmetic so participants can concentrate on the reasoning.

9. Take the detective method beyond medicine

💻 IT & cybersecurity

A fraud detector flags transactions. If actual fraud is rare, even a small false-positive rate can send many legitimate transactions for review.

🤝 Social welfare

An eligibility system flags potentially ineligible claims. False positives may wrongly delay support for people who qualify; false negatives may miss improper claims.

📣 Marketing

A campaign identifies likely buyers. A positive prediction is not proof of purchase intent. Base rates and the cost of false leads matter.

🌱 Development

A programme screens households for vulnerability. A useful system needs to consider missed households, incorrectly flagged households and the consequences of each error.

🏥 Clinical medicine

Interpret a laboratory result alongside symptoms, risk factors, examination, test limitations and appropriate confirmatory testing.

📊 Data science

Accuracy alone can mislead when classes are imbalanced. Inspect false positives, false negatives, precision, recall and the consequences of errors.

Universal lesson: a detector does not answer “Is this person truly in the target group?” until you consider both the test's performance and how common the target condition is.

10. Conference challenge

  1. Set prevalence to 1%, sensitivity to 95% and specificity to 95%. Investigate 1,000 people.
  2. Without changing sensitivity or specificity, increase prevalence to 20%. What happens to PPV?
  3. Return to 1% prevalence and improve specificity to 99%. How many false positives remain?
  4. Increase the population to 10,000. Why does the percentage stay similar while the number of false positives grows?
  5. Explain why a positive screening result is not automatically a diagnosis.

Don't ask only, “How accurate is the test?” Ask, “What does this result mean for this person in this population?”

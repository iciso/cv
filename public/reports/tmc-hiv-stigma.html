🎯 The mission

A patient living with HIV comes to hospital for treatment. The clinical team must make decisions about surgery, protective equipment, cleaning, ward placement, and occupational exposure.

Your mission is to choose care that is clinically appropriate, safe, respectful, and evidence-based.

Three principles to remember
1. HIV status alone is not a reason to deny or unnecessarily delay indicated surgery.
2. Standard Precautions apply to every patient.
3. Respectful care and proper infection prevention go together.

This educational simulation does not replace local hospital policy, specialist advice, or individual clinical assessment.

🧠 Pocket definitions

PLHIV

People living with HIV. This language recognises the person before the medical condition.

ART

Antiretroviral therapy: medicines that suppress HIV and help people live longer, healthier lives.

CD4 count

A blood test that helps assess immune function. The result is one part of a person's clinical assessment.

Viral load: TND

“Target Not Detected” means HIV RNA was not detected by that test at its detection limit. It does not mean the virus has been eradicated from the body.

Standard Precautions

Routine infection-prevention measures for every patient. They include hand hygiene, safe sharps handling, and PPE selected according to anticipated exposure.

U=U

Undetectable = Untransmittable: sustained viral suppression prevents sexual transmission of HIV. Do not extend this slogan to claim zero risk from occupational needlestick or blood exposure.

❓ Quick revision: frequently asked questions

1. Can a person living with HIV undergo surgery for a fibroid?

Yes, if the procedure is clinically indicated and the individual assessment supports it. A patient taking ART with suppressed viral load and a CD4 count above 200 cells/mm³ can generally proceed with the planned surgery as a patient without HIV, while receiving the same ordinary perioperative assessment. The gynaecological indication, other medical conditions, medications, and surgical risks still matter.

2. Does a TND viral load mean occupational exposure is impossible?

No. TND is reassuring evidence of viral suppression, and sustained suppression prevents sexual transmission. It should not be used to declare occupational blood exposure risk to be zero. If a needlestick or other qualifying exposure occurs, follow the occupational exposure protocol promptly.

3. Should an “HIV kit” be used for every patient known to have HIV?

HIV status alone is not a reason for a separate PPE kit. Use the PPE indicated by the procedure and anticipated exposure under Standard Precautions. If local policy specifies equipment for a genuine procedural hazard, follow that policy for the relevant hazard—not as a stigma-based ritual.

4. Must the operating theatre be specially disinfected after a PLHIV case?

Routine cleaning, environmental disinfection, and instrument reprocessing should follow the same validated infection-prevention procedures used after other patients. Clean and disinfect based on contamination and the procedure, not solely on HIV status.

5. Does HIV status alone require an isolated ward?

No. HIV alone does not justify isolation. Isolation or additional precautions may be needed for a separate clinical reason, such as another infection requiring transmission-based precautions.

6. What if a healthcare worker gets a needlestick?

Immediately follow the facility's exposure protocol: wash the area with soap and water, report the exposure promptly, obtain urgent occupational-health assessment, and start HIV post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) as soon as indicated. Do not wait for symptoms. Follow local testing and follow-up recommendations.

7. What about a patient whose HIV status is unknown?

Apply Standard Precautions to every patient, regardless of known or unknown status. Testing and consent should follow applicable policy. Do not rely on appearance, assumptions, or disclosure to decide who receives safe care.

8. What is the difference between universal safety and HIV-specific fear?

Universal safety means using appropriate precautions consistently for everyone. HIV-specific fear means adding unnecessary restrictions solely because of a diagnosis. The first protects people; the second can waste resources and cause stigma.
